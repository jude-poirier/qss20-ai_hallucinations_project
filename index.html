Brief for the Analyst

Questions Presented (research question)

Whether the severity of the sanction a court imposes for a fabricated, AI-generated citation depends on whether the responsible filer is an attorney, a self-represented litigant, or a judge.

And whether, holding the features of the case constant, the identity of the filer predicts the court's response better than the conduct itself.

Summary of the Argument (abstract)

This brief follows the project end to end: the question, the record it rests on, the method, the result, and what it means. It does, and the record is not close. Attorneys draw a personal penalty, discipline or money, in close to 38 percent of their cases. Self-represented litigants mostly receive a warning. When the fabrication comes from the bench, the court corrects the ruling and imposes nothing on the judge. An ordered logistic regression places a pro se filer's odds of a higher sanction tier at 0.32 against a counseled filer, holding case features constant.2 A model given those case features can barely predict severity at all, which returns the weight of the explanation to who filed rather than what was filed.

Statement of the Case (data and methods)

The AI record is the AI Hallucination Cases Database, a curated catalog of court cases involving AI-fabricated material, comprising 1,848 cases of which 1,279 arise in the United States.1 A non-AI comparison group was drawn from CourtListener bulk data by streaming a 2.3 GB file and keeping 2023 to 2026 sanctions rulings, with criminal, licensing, and bar-discipline matters removed. Each case was assigned a responsible actor and a severity tier from 0 (no sanction) to 4 (professional or terminal). The coding was validated against an independent hand-coded sample at Cohen's kappa 0.87 (Exhibit A).2

Argument (methods and results)

I. The responses form an accountability gradient. (descriptive proportions by actor)

Sorted by the worst consequence on record, the three actors line up cleanly. Attorneys absorb the professional and monetary penalties, about 18 percent professional discipline and 20 percent monetary. Pro se litigants sit at the warning tier and draw professional discipline in zero percent of cases, since there is no bar to discipline them. Judges see the ruling undone and face nothing themselves.

II. The comparison survives regression. (ordered logistic regression, court-clustered standard errors)

An ordered logit of severity on representation, with standard errors clustered by court, sets the pro se odds ratio at 0.32 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.41), written from scratch in NumPy and SciPy. About 52 percent of pro se filers land at the warning tier, against roughly 21 percent of counseled filers, while counseled cases carry the bulk of the procedural, monetary, and professional outcomes. No legal field separates from the baseline, and severity trends down across the window rather than up.

III. The case itself does not predict the outcome. (supervised classification with permutation importance)

A classifier trained to recover severity from case features reaches about 0.45 accuracy against a 0.39 baseline and defaults to predicting a warning for nearly everything. Only pro se status and whether the tool was named carry measurable weight. The negative result is the point, since it locates the explanation in the identity of the filer.

IV. The AI-versus-non-AI comparison, with a caution. (pooled OLS regression)

Pooling the arms allows a direct comparison of severity. On average the AI cases score higher (Exhibit G), and the pooled model puts a positive coefficient on the AI indicator (Exhibit H). Read both as provisional. The two arms are assembled on different terms: the AI arm is curated for cases where a sanction question was already adjudicated, while the controls are trigger-matched opinions that are largely non-events. That base-rate difference sits in the study design, not the coding, and it inflates the AI arm's mean no matter how well each case is scored. The labels themselves are reliable (Exhibit A); it is the comparison of the two arms that carries the caveat.3

Conclusion (discussion and takeaway)

The size of the gap carries the finding. Among counseled filers a personal penalty, meaning professional discipline or a monetary sanction, lands in close to 38 percent of cases, with professional discipline alone at about 18 percent. Among pro se filers those categories nearly vanish, and about 52 percent receive no more than a warning. The ordered logit fixes the difference at a pro se odds ratio of 0.32 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.41), holding legal field, court level, and filing year constant, and the estimate holds after clustering by court. Severity trends down across the 2023 to 2026 window, so the gradient is not courts growing harsher over time, and a classifier given the case features reaches only about 0.45 accuracy against a 0.39 baseline.

So who gets punished comes down to whether the filer is a lawyer. Courts have real tools for lawyers: bar referrals, discipline, fee awards. For a filer who is not a lawyer there is very little a court can reach for. Most fake AI citations still come from lawyers, and if that shifts toward self-represented filers, the courts will be left facing the group they can do the least about. The analyst respectfully submits that the record supports this finding.
