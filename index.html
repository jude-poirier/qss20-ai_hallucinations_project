Re: Whether a court's response to an AI-fabricated citation depends on who filed it

I. Question Presented

Whether the severity of the sanction a court imposes for a fabricated, AI-generated citation depends on whether the responsible filer is an attorney, a self-represented (pro se) litigant, or a judge.

II. Brief Answer

Yes, and sharply. The three kinds of filers meet three different responses. Attorneys draw personal penalties, discipline or money, in close to half of their cases. Pro se litigants usually receive a warning and little more. When the fabrication comes from the bench, the court corrects the ruling and imposes nothing on the judge. Holding case features constant, a pro se filer has about a third the odds of an attorney of reaching a higher sanction tier.2 The pattern is best read as a feature of the sanction system itself: its instruments are built around members of the bar, so a responsible party outside the bar leaves little for the system to act on.

III. Statement of Facts

The AI record is drawn from the AI Hallucination Cases Database, a hand-curated catalog of court cases involving AI-fabricated material, comprising 1,848 cases of which 1,279 arise in the United States.1 A non-AI comparison group was assembled from CourtListener bulk data by streaming a 2.3 GB file of opinion clusters and retaining 2023 to 2026 rulings on sanctions questions, with criminal, licensing-board, and standalone bar-discipline matters removed.

Each case was assigned a responsible actor and a severity tier from 0 to 4, where 0 is no sanction, 1 a warning, 2 a procedural or corrective order, 3 a monetary penalty, and 4 a professional or terminal consequence such as a bar referral or dismissal as a sanction. The coding was validated against independent hand-coding.2

IV. Analysis

A. The responses form an accountability gradient.

Sorting every case by the most serious personal consequence on record produces a clear ordering across the three actors. Attorneys absorb the professional and monetary penalties. Pro se litigants cluster at the warning tier. Judges see the ruling undone while they themselves are left alone.

B. The litigant comparison survives regression.

An ordered logistic regression of severity on representation, with standard errors clustered by court, places the pro se odds ratio at 0.32 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.41). The regression and its clustered errors were written directly in NumPy and SciPy. No legal field separates from the baseline.

C. Severity is not recoverable from the case itself.

A supervised classifier trained to predict severity from case features does little better than always guessing the most common outcome. It defaults to predicting a warning for nearly everything. The only variables carrying real weight are whether the filer was pro se and whether the AI tool was named. The negative result is itself the point: severity does not follow from the content of the case, which returns the emphasis to who the actor is.

D. The AI-versus-non-AI comparison, with a caution.

Pooling the AI cases with the non-AI controls permits a direct comparison of severity by arm. That comparison should be read with care, for the reason set out in the footnote: the two arms are assembled on different terms, and that difference sits in the study design rather than in the coding.3

V. Conclusion

The court's sanction machinery is calibrated to lawyers. When the party responsible for a fabricated citation sits outside the bar, the system has few instruments to reach, and the same underlying conduct meets a materially different response according to the identity of the filer. Any reform aimed at AI-fabricated filings will have to build a response for non-bar actors that the current apparatus does not contain.

Jude Poirier
