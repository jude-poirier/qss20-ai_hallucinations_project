## Figures

### `figures/fig_accountability_gradient.png`
**The headline.** Stacked bars showing, for each responsible actor — lawyers (n=719), pro se
litigants (n=1,079), judges (n=27) — the share of cases ending in each consequence type:
professional discipline, monetary penalty, a case/ruling remedy, a warning, or nothing recorded.

The three actors are met by different responses. Lawyers draw personal penalties (discipline +
money) in ~44% of cases. Pro se litigants are mostly warned. When a judge is the source, the
modal response is undoing the ruling rather than touching the judge. Uses the full database
(all countries) so the small judge group has enough cases to show at all — the judge cell is
indicative only.

### `figures/fig_severity_dist.png`
Observed sanction-severity distribution (tiers 0–4) for pro se (n=763) vs. counseled (n=496)
U.S. cases. Pro se cases pile up at tier 1 (warning); counseled cases spread into the
procedural, monetary, and professional/terminal tiers.

### `figures/fig_forest.png`
Ordered logistic regression of severity on representation and controls — odds ratios with
court-clustered 95% CIs on a log scale. `pro_se` sits near 0.32 for reaching a higher severity
tier; federal courts trend slightly higher; severity trends down over time (`year_c`); no legal
field is distinguishable from the baseline.

*Note:* per reviewer feedback the project is moving toward a continuous regression with a
beta-coefficient forest. This figure is the ordered-logit version.

### `figures/fig_ml_importance.png`
Permutation importance (drop in macro-F1) from a scikit-learn classifier predicting severity.
`pro_se` and `tool_named` carry most of the signal; legal field and year contribute little or
nothing. Read this as "which features matter," not as evidence of a strong predictor — see the
confusion matrix.

### `figures/fig_ml_confusion.png`
Row-normalized confusion matrix for that classifier. It predicts "warning" for almost
everything (the warning row is 0.86; other rows leak heavily into the warning column), so
severity is only weakly predictable from these case features.

The takeaway is a **negative result**, and it is the interesting one: sanction severity is
largely not a function of observable case characteristics, which reinforces that *who the actor
is* matters more than what the case contains.

## Caveats — apply to every figure

- **Associational, not causal.** Representation is not randomly assigned.
- **Keyword-coded severity.** The 0–4 scale is a rule-based reading of the `Outcome` field and
  still needs hand-validation via `code/labeling/validate_coding.py`.
- **Detected cases only.** The database records fabrications that were caught; undetected ones
  are invisible, and nothing here can bound how many.
- **Judges: n=27** (8 U.S.), so that part of the gradient is a first look, not an estimate.
